Project03 of 04

Oogway, a robotic sea turtle

A bio-mimetic underwater robot that swims with flapping fins and dives by breathing water in and out of a syringe, the way a turtle adjusts its buoyancy.

My roleCAD, 3D printing, build, testing and debugging
CourseME 366, Electro-Mechanical System Design
TeamTeam Oblivious, four members
Tested atIWFM water reservoir, BUET, July 2025

The idea01

Borrowed from a turtle.

Most small underwater robots stack thrusters. Sea turtles swim and dive with far less. We copied the animal, part for part.

  1. TurtleFront flippersMain source of thrust.
    OogwayTwo-axis front fins3D printed, each driven by two servos for roll and yaw.
  2. TurtleRear flippersSteering and balance.
    OogwayLight rear legsOne servo each, kept light to save weight.
  3. TurtleLungsAdjust buoyancy to rise or sink.
    OogwaySyringe ballastA linear actuator pulls water in to dive and pushes it out to rise.
  4. TurtleShellProtection with a smooth outline.
    OogwayFoam carapaceLight EVA foam covers that also help the robot float.

The system03

What's inside.

One sealed acrylic hull carries the brain, the power and the ballast.

HullAcrylic cylinder, 10 by 40 cmThreaded nylon end caps
ActuationSix servo motorsFour on the front fins, two on the rear legs
Ballast50 mL syringeDriven by a 12 V linear actuator
ControlArduino UnoPython GUI on a 10 m tether
Power11.1 V LiPo batteryStepped down to 5 V for the servos
Depth testedUp to 1.5 mLimited by the reservoir

Buoyancy04

Breathing to dive.

Oogway is trimmed to float slightly. Pulling water into the syringe makes it heavier than the water around it, so it sinks. Pushing the water out lets it rise. Try it.

Syringe empty: Oogway floats at the surface.

Iterations05

Three hulls, one keeper.

We proved the buoyancy system first, then built the body around it.

  1. Hull 1Soda bottle, phone over Wi-Fi

    ProblemIt dived, but had no fail-safe. If the surface command dropped, the robot stayed down.

  2. Hull 2Five-litre water jug

    ProblemFar too buoyant. It needed about 4.5 kg of extra ballast just to sink.

  3. FinalAcrylic cylinder on a tether

    FixCompact and watertight, with a wired link so the robot can always be called back.

Waterproofing06

Keeping the water out.

Three places leaked. Each got its own fix.

Weak pointEnd cap holes

Where the tether and wires pass through the caps.

  • Epoxy resin
  • Hot glue
Weak pointCylinder threads

The screw joint between each cap and the hull.

  • Petroleum jelly
  • Nylon glue
Weak pointServo motors

Opened, packed with petroleum jelly inside, then sealed outside.

  • Petroleum jelly
  • B-6000 glue
No significant leaksThe hull stayed dry through repeated dives in the reservoir.

In the water07

It swims.

Tested in the reservoirs of the Institute of Water and Flood Management (IWFM), BUET.

Swam forwardSteady thrust from the flapping front fins.
Turned both waysBy flapping one fin differently from the other.
Dove and surfacedThrough up to 1.5 m, with smooth transitions.
HoveredHeld a set depth once calibrated.
What did not go perfectly: play in the fin joints made turns slower and less precise than we wanted. That is the first thing a second version would fix.

Control08

A button for every move.

A Python interface sends simple commands down the tether. The Arduino turns each one into servo angles or actuator travel.
